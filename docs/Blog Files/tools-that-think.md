# Tools That Think: A Course Series Built Around Instruments I Still Have to Buy

On October 14 I left a short note in my LMS repo. It listed a slide rule, an abacus, maps and a compass, with an emphasis on analog tools that would still be useful without electricity, and it asked for a list of courses like those.

Four courses later, none of them is released. They are written and checked, and they are waiting on two things: me, with the tools in my hands on camera, and reviewers I have not found yet. This is the first of four posts about them.

## Why a tool is a way into the math

A slide rule does not hide its math. It is the math. Every number on its scale sits at a distance from 1 proportional to its logarithm, so sliding one length along another adds two logarithms, and adding logarithms multiplies the numbers. Charles Pickworth's 1920 manual called the instrument "in effect a concisely arranged table of logarithms" (Pickworth, 1920, p. 5). A student who has never trusted a logarithm can hold one.

The other tools work the same way. A compass turns an angle into something you walk. The wildland fire workbook my compass course leans on puts the stakes in one line: "1 degree off can result in 920 feet off course in 10 miles" (National Wildfire Coordinating Group [NWCG], 2016, p. 86). A surveyor's chain turns area into counting. One chain is 66 feet and 100 links, 80 chains make a mile, and 10 square chains make an acre (Bureau of Land Management [BLM], 2009, table 2-1, p. 37). Drag a chain across a field once and the acre stops being a word.

That is the bet behind the series. A phone gives you the answer. These tools make you build it, one length or one angle at a time.

## What the catalog already had

Before anything was written, the agent searched every course file in the catalog for these tools. None of them was taught anywhere. The few hits were history or passing mentions.

One hit was wrong in a useful way. The first candidate list said my golf course mentioned Gunter's chain, the surveyor's chain. It does not. The "Gunter" in that course is Madison Gunter, a golfer. The surveying brief caught it and the list was corrected. A keyword match is a lead, not a fact.

## Four decisions

The candidate list came back with twenty tools and tool families, each marked as a possible course or a lesson inside one. I made four decisions the same day.

1. **One series across categories,** called Tools That Think, code TOOLS. Each course lists on its own shelf: map and compass under Survival, the slide rule and the surveying tools under Science & Math, land descriptions under Civics. The series ties them together.
2. **An order.** Map and compass first, then the slide rule, then surveying. The rest of the list: the abacus, the sextant, the instruments for reading the sky (planisphere, astrolabe, sundial), compass and straightedge, weather instruments, and precise measuring with calipers, a micrometer and a balance.
3. **Small tools become lessons.** Napier's bones, printed log tables, nomograms and the pilot's E6B went into the slide rule course. The planimeter went into surveying.
4. **I buy the tools.** Every hands-on lesson is marked P, for a demonstration I record myself. The written lesson still teaches the whole operation, so a learner without the video can follow it.

## Brief first, then build

No course in my catalog gets built before I approve its brief: what a learner will be able to do, a draft outline, the existing lessons it connects to, whether it should be a course at all, and the evidence for every claim, each marked verified, reported or unverified. Three briefs came back on October 14. I answered every question and approved all three that day.

My answers changed the courses. I asked each one for a lesson on what to look for, in a compass, a slide rule, a surveyor's tools and even a land description, with no brand endorsements. I brought night navigation and difficult terrain into the compass course. And I split surveying into two courses, which gets its own post.

All four are public and free, and all four show "Coming soon" until their holds clear. Every lesson that needs someone says so at the top, in a box that begins "Before release." The courses sit on branches in the LMS repo, waiting to be bundled, and merging them still releases nothing.

The slide rule is the only one with no reviewer in its hold. It is mathematics and history, every worked example comes from public-domain sources or plain arithmetic, and it has already been through a full verification pass. The other three teach things where a mistake costs more. A wrong bearing or declination sign can strand a walker. A misread deed can put up a fence in the wrong place or start a dispute.

## What comes next

The abacus is next. I approved its brief on October 15, and it will be the fifth course in the series. The tools for all of them are on my list to buy, and when they arrive, I film.

Each course gets its own post: why the compass course is built from a 2001 Army manual instead of the current one, how the slide rule course teaches a priority dispute from the 1630s, and why the surveying course became two.

## Three things worth stealing

**Teach the tool that makes the math visible.** A logarithm is abstract until it is a length you can slide. An angle is abstract until it is the difference between a trail and a swamp. Pick the instrument where the idea is the mechanism.

**Search before you build, and check the hits.** The coverage search found nothing that taught these tools, which made the case for building. It also found a "Gunter" in a golf course. It was the wrong Gunter. A match is where checking starts.

**Write down who has to say yes before you write the lesson.** Every risky lesson in these courses names the person who must read it before release. The course cannot ship around that line, and the line exists before the person does.
