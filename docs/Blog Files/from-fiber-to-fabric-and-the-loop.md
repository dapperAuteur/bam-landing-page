# From Fiber to Fabric, and the Loop Back to the Garden

On 2026-10-05 I asked for two things: a crochet and string-making course to go with my knot-tying course, and a course on the manure of different animals, composting, and using both to grow food, with the animals that help a garden along the way. Later that day I added braiding for hair and yarn, and raising animals for yarn.

None of that went straight into a course file. In my LMS, a course starts as a brief I review: what it would teach, what already covers it, whether it should be a course at all, and the status of every factual claim behind it. The coverage search decides a lot. Here it decided that my requests were really two connected series.

## Two series

**From Fiber to Fabric** follows a strand from plant or animal to cloth:

1. **Making String**: fiber to cord.
2. **Knot-Tying & Rope Work**: what to do with the cord. It already existed, and joined the series on 2026-10-08.
3. **Crocheting**: a single strand worked into fabric with a hook.

**The Calorie Loop** follows food and waste around a small farm:

1. **Manure and Compost** comes first.
2. Two slots are kept open for growing trees and growing protein. Their briefs come after the manure course ships.
3. **Raising Animals for Yarn** comes last, and it also closes the fiber line. A course can belong to only one series, so it sits in the loop, is listed under Trade Skills as well, and names the fiber line in its own text.

String got its own course, rather than a chapter in the crochet course, because the planning found it is the hub: knots, crochet, braiding and fiber animals all draw on it. Braiding is approved, but it waits on demonstrations I still have to record myself.

## What each course teaches

**Making String** (19 lessons) covers how plant and animal fibers are prepared, twisting and plying cord by hand from a U.S. Army manual's method and drawing, ropewalks, and the archaeology of string. Two of its choices I like:

- **It never gives a safe working load.** The course's own rule is that hand-made cord is never load-bearing for people, and it states that as its own rule instead of pinning it on a source.
- **Where the record is a dispute, it teaches the dispute.** One claim about very old flax fibers is presented as an argument between two papers, not as a settled fact.

**Crocheting** (32 lessons) covers tools and yarn, the chain and the basic stitches, rows and rounds, shaping, reading a pattern, finishing, the documented history, and the connection to hyperbolic geometry. It is honest about a thin record. The magic ring has no source the research could find, and the lesson says so instead of filling the gap from memory. One fix came from checking the series against itself: the brief assumed the knot course taught the slip knot. It does not, so Crocheting teaches it, from a 1918 book on knitting and crocheting.

**Manure and Compost** (22 lessons) covers manures by animal from federal conservation handbooks, composting methods and the science behind them, and using both in gardens and on farms: soil tests, timing, runoff, and two federal rules. Its Indiana section is built from Purdue Extension guides and state rules, and ends with an assignment: find your county office and its compost guidance. Its refusals are content, not disclaimers:

- **No home application rate** in pounds per square foot, because no source in its research gives one. It teaches the soil test and sends you to your extension office.
- **The 90 and 120 days are taught as the organic rule's intervals, not as food-safety law**, because the FDA's produce safety rule leaves its matching paragraph "[Reserved]." Both rules are shown side by side.
- **No composting of human waste.** It is regulated and out of scope, and the lesson says so.

**Raising Animals for Yarn** (28 lessons) covers sheep, alpacas and llamas, and yaks in its first version: what each needs, health and welfare, fleece from shearing day to yarn, the federal scrapie program, the Indiana law a keeper runs into, and history, including the federal livestock reduction on the Navajo Nation. It never tells a learner how to shear, lamb, castrate, dock, medicate or treat an animal. Those belong to a veterinarian, a shearer or a mentor, and the lessons say so where it matters.

Between them, the four new courses carry just over 2,000 quiz questions.

## How the sources were sorted

Every source was sorted into a tier before it was used. The tier is decided per source, not per course.

- **A public-domain backbone, free to host.** Verrill's 1917 knot book on Project Gutenberg. U.S. Army manuals marked "Approved for public release; distribution is unlimited." Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century needlework books. Federal conservation handbooks, federal regulations, and George Washington Carver's bulletins. King's *Farmers of Forty Centuries* and Darwin, both on Gutenberg. For the fiber animals, federal statistics, the scrapie program's documents, and USDA wool leaflets from 1933 and 1977.
- **Freely readable but copyrighted: linked, never copied.** Purdue Extension's guides, the Craft Yarn Council's pages, Indiana agency pages, and news reports.
- **Copyrighted and behind a paywall: cited only.** A few journal papers fall here, and where only an abstract was read, the course file records that.

Then there are my own books. The plan allowed purchased books exactly one job: a topic map, a reminder of what a course on the subject usually covers, never extracted or paraphrased into a course file. In the finished courses they did even less than that. Making String, Crocheting, Manure and Compost, and Raising Animals for Yarn each say in their headers that my purchased books are not used. The older knot-tying course uses one copyrighted guide only to confirm its modern topic list, and names it as further reading.

Hosting a figure is publishing it, so only figures whose rights allow it went up: 22 from the fiber courses' sources were uploaded and placed in lessons.

## Checking, and two holds

The two fiber courses and Raising Animals for Yarn went through the same pipeline: an author working only from the verified research file, a checker for each section with a skeptic behind it, and a quiz pass with a reviewer for each bank. Raising Animals for Yarn took 90 claim fixes and 5 quiz fixes, plus 16 research checks for the hedges its lessons carry. Making String and Crocheting together got 13 research checks. The quiz pass on those two is also where the shortest-answer tell was first noticed, which became [its own post](/blog/fixing-one-quiz-tell-created-its-opposite).

One more pass came after that. The series started life as "From Fibre to Fabric." I made American spelling a rule across all my apps, and 682 words in four courses were converted, with a check proving every quotation was left exactly as its source printed it.

Two courses are held until a qualified reviewer has read them. Manure and Compost waits for a Master Gardener or an extension educator, and I am still finding one. Raising Animals for Yarn waits for a shepherd or fiber farmer to review its husbandry lessons. Pathogens, food safety, animal health and local law are not things I sign off on alone.

## Three things worth stealing

**Let the coverage search shape the catalog before anything is written.** Four requests became two series and a clear order because of a search, not a whim.

**Sort sources per source, and write the tier down.** A course can rest on a public-domain backbone and still lean on a copyrighted page it may only link. Deciding that up front is what keeps a free page from quietly becoming a copied one.

**Write the refusal into the lesson.** "No application rate, because no source gives one" teaches the learner something true about the subject. Silence would have taught them nothing, and a guess would have taught them something false.
