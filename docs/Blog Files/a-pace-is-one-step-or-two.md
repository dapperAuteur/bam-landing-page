# A Pace Is One Step or Two. My Compass Course Teaches Both.

Map and Compass: Finding Your Way on Land is the first course in [Tools That Think](/blog/tools-that-think), my series on analog tools with math built into them. It has 36 lessons in eight sections and 700 quiz questions. It is written, and it is not released.

This is about the two documents it is built from, the places they disagree, and the four places near Indianapolis it tells you to practice.

## The obvious source was off limits

The obvious backbone for a land navigation course is the Army's manual, *Map Reading and Land Navigation*. The research for my course read the covers of its recent editions before anything was written. The current edition, a training circular from 2013, restricts distribution to U.S. government agencies and their contractors, on its own cover. The 2005 edition carries the same restriction.

The 2001 edition does not. Its cover reads: "DISTRIBUTION RESTRICTION: Approved for public release; distribution is unlimited" (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 2001, cover).

All three are federal works, so copyright was never the problem. The distribution statement was. Copies of the restricted editions circulate online, and that does not change what their covers say. So the course never quotes, links or hosts the 2005 or 2013 editions. Lesson 1 names them once, to say why they are not used.

The cost is that the course quotes superseded doctrine, and it says so. When a lesson quotes the 2001 manual, it is quoting old doctrine, chosen because anyone may read it.

## The second source fills the gap

The 2001 manual teaches the lensatic compass. It does not teach the baseplate compass most hikers buy. For that, the course reads a second public document beside it: the National Wildfire Coordinating Group's *Basic Land Navigation*, a 2016 workbook for wildland fire crews. Its notice is plain: "All other NWCG information is in the public domain" (National Wildfire Coordinating Group [NWCG], 2016, p. iii).

Two documents mean two vocabularies.

## Where the two disagree

The sharpest example is the pace. The Army manual: "A pace is equal to one natural step, about 30 inches long" (Headquarters, Department of the Army, 2001, p. 5-11). The workbook: "A pace is defined as the average length of two natural steps (a count is made each time the same foot touches the ground)" (NWCG, 2016, p. 133).

Neither is wrong. They count different things, and a walker who mixes them is off by a factor of two. The course works the arithmetic: 30 inches is about 0.76 meters, so 100 meters is roughly 131 one-step paces, or about 66 two-step paces. Then it gives one rule. Decide which you count, write it on your route card, and never mix them. My demonstration for that lesson, still to be filmed, calibrates my own pace both ways, on flat ground and uphill.

The pace is not the only clash. Finding your own position from landmarks is "resection" in the Army manual and "triangulation" in the workbook, which uses the same word for locating a distant point, what the Army calls "intersection." Lesson 22 teaches both vocabularies side by side, so a learner can talk to someone trained either way.

The declination diagram is the third. The Army builds its diagram around the angle between grid north and magnetic north. The workbook and the USGS work from true north to magnetic north. Mix the two and you get a sign error, which is the exact mistake the course exists to prevent.

## Every number carries a date

Maps age and magnetic north moves, so every figure in the course that can go stale carries its date.

The Hindustan quad, which covers Morgan-Monroe State Forest, prints a declination of 4°28' west, labeled for 2019. The course's own calculation from the current World Magnetic Model gives about 4°47' west for the center of that sheet on October 14, 2026. That was the course's own estimate, so it went on the list of facts to confirm. The fact-check confirmed it: NOAA's online calculator, on its current model, gives 4.78 degrees west for that sheet and that day, which is 4°47', give or take 0.37 degrees (National Centers for Environmental Information [NCEI], n.d.). The check is closed, and the lesson still tells learners to use the calculator on the day they walk.

## Four places to practice near Indianapolis

I asked for real public land near home, with the rules that apply at each. Lesson 3 names four, nearest first, each on a free US Topo map, with the rules quoted from each manager's own page or map as read on October 14, 2026.

- **Fort Harrison State Park,** on the Indianapolis East quad, though its northern edge runs off the top of the sheet. The trail map says "For safety, please stay on marked trails," and two of its four nature preserves are "Closed to the public" (Indiana Department of Natural Resources [DNR], 2026). Good for reading the map against the ground, pace counts on a measured stretch and bearings to landmarks. Not for leaving the trail.
- **Morgan-Monroe State Forest,** on the Hindustan quad. The forest built a compass course, the Pathfinder Orienteering Trail, and lists it as closed after an EF-2 tornado hit the forest on June 18, 2026. The clear way to be there after dark is as a registered backcountry camper: "All campers must register" (DNR, n.d.).
- **Yellowwood State Forest,** on the Belmont quad. The forest's web page and its property map disagree about camping along the Tecumseh Trail, so the lesson quotes both and tells you to ask the forest office which one governs.
- **The Charles C. Deam Wilderness** in the Hoosier National Forest, on the Elkinsville quad.

## The one place that expects you off the trail after dark

Of the four, only the Deam Wilderness has posted rules that expect a walker off the trail and out overnight. "Horse riders must stay on designated trails," and nothing like that is said of walkers. The national forest's general rules say it outright: "Hikers may hike anywhere except where an area is posted closed" (USDA Forest Service, n.d.-b). Camping is prohibited "within 200 feet of trails, trailheads, roads, water sources, rock shelters, caves, and historic structures, unless in a Forest Service designated campsite." "Group size is limited to 10 persons or less." And the page carries the warning a navigation course is built around: "Please get a map before visiting this area, as the boundaries are not clearly marked" (USDA Forest Service, n.d.-a).

That matters because night navigation and difficult terrain were not in the first draft. The brief named them and taught neither. I brought both in, and lessons 30 and 31 point to the Deam. The night lesson keeps the Army's methods: work out every bearing in daylight, expect shorter steps and a different pace count, keep steering marks close together. It adds the civilian question first. Are you allowed to be there at all?

One rule applies to me. Before I film a demonstration on state land, I ask the property whether my filming needs the DNR's commercial film agreement.

## What it is not, and what it waits for

The course says plainly that it is not a search-and-rescue course, not wilderness first aid and not a safety qualification.

It is public, free and held as "Coming soon" until two things happen. A qualified reviewer, a search-and-rescue member or a wilderness or land-navigation instructor, reads the eight lessons marked for one: correcting for declination, following a bearing, the turn-back rule, a planned route walked and logged, what to do when you are lost, night navigation, difficult terrain, and where GPS fails. And I record the demonstrations in seventeen lessons, with my own compasses on my own quad. No reviewer is named yet. How a reviewer's sign-off attaches to exactly the lessons they read is in [an earlier post](/blog/reviewer-name-on-what-they-read).

## Three things worth stealing

**Read the cover before the contents.** The best-known source was off limits because of one line on its cover, and a copy online does not change that line. Check the distribution statement and the license before you build on a document.

**When two good sources disagree, teach the disagreement.** A pace is one step or two. Hiding that would leave a learner off by half the first time they met someone trained the other way.

**Date every number that can go stale, and flag your own estimates.** A declination printed for 2019 is not today's declination, and a figure you computed yourself is not the agency's until someone checks it.
