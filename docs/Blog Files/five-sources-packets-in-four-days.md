# Five Sources Packets in Four Days, and What a Packet Is

On 2026-11-10 I gave my course-building agents a new rule: "provide sources to me/teacher before using it in course creation for me/teacher to approve." It became gate D5c in the Learn.WitUS course-creation rubric. Before any lesson is written, every source the course will cite goes to the course's owner as a packet.

Between 2026-11-13 and 2026-11-16, five packets were written, plus a catalog of my ebook library. None of them has produced a lesson yet, and that is the design.

## What a packet is

Each source gets an entry with:

- its APA 7 reference;
- its type;
- how the course would use it;
- a perspective line, so I know whose point of view it carries;
- access: whether the course can host it, link it or only cite it;
- a provenance note and the checks it passed;
- the date it was opened.

Then come the sections that make it a decision document instead of a bibliography: **Where the sources disagree**, **What I could not open**, **Open questions**, three possible shapes for the course, and an approval task in my queue. Each packet is rendered to PDF and EPUB, because I read on an e-reader.

## The five

**Backlash in American politics.** 50 sources: the overthrow of Reconstruction, the 21st century, status threat theory, polarization, parallels in other countries, and teaching aids. The Abrajano and Hajnal book was read with page numbers and is cited as the 2015 Princeton edition, because that is what its copyright page shows. The file on my disk said 2017. A course built from the filename would have been wrong in a reference.

**Bethel AME Church in Champaign, Illinois.** 41 sources, built to prepare me for a conversation with the church's pastor. The packet found that the church's whole early online record traces to one 1991 church history. Whether the church was founded in 1863 or 1864 is unsettled. It has no landmark status. The packet ends with 15 to 25 questions to bring, and a consent note: Illinois requires every party's consent to record, so I ask before anything is recorded.

**Military tactics.** 55 sources. The course studies decision-making and history, never instruction in violence. One finding: "no plan survives contact" traces to a paraphrase of Moltke's 1871 essay, not to Clausewitz.

**Building coalitions across class, race and culture.** 55 sources, from the Virginia Readjusters and North Carolina Fusion through labor and the civil rights era, 21 theory sources, COINTELPRO, and current practice. One of the COINTELPRO sources is a 1968 FBI memo from my own files. Three sources are shared with the backlash packet.

**The Speedway revision.** The fifth packet answers a museum's review of an existing course: the town of Speedway, the museum's facts, a contested first, standards and careers. It goes through the same approval before the new unit is written.

**And the library.** Not a packet, but the same discipline: a catalog of my ebook library, 1,381 files, 677 of them books, 402 opened to their title pages. With it came a report proposing 15 courses, ranked by how much of each is already on disk.

## What the packets caught

Each of these would have gone into a course as a plain statement if the sources had gone straight to lessons:

- **Two of the library's books carry download-site stamps on their pages.** A course assigns a clean copy, not the one on my disk.
- **Library of Congress, Justia, Oyez, PNAS, the Marine Corps site, Project Gutenberg and the FBI Vault refuse automated fetching.** Those sources were confirmed through other records and listed under "What I could not open," each with how a person can get it.
- **No opened source states the "half a million visitors" figure a reviewer cited.** Until someone finds the source, the figure stays out.
- **A children's read-along in the library is a fact-check exercise, not a source.** It simplifies past accuracy in places, which makes it useful for a class to check and wrong to cite.

## Why "What I could not open" matters

A research assistant that hands you the links it could not visit, says why, and says how a person can get each one is more useful than one that pretends it read them. That list is a deliverable, not a failure. It tells me which half hour of my own reading turns a "probably" into a fact, and it keeps an agent from filling a blocked page with what the page probably says.

The packet also moves the argument about sources to the point where it is cheap. Arguing about a 1991 church history costs a reply to a packet. Arguing about it after twelve lessons quote it costs twelve rewrites, a re-record, and whatever trust a reader spent on the first version.

Nothing is built from any of the five until I approve it.

## What this cost

**To build:** five helper runs on Claude Opus 5.5. The backlash packet is from 2026-11-13 and is priced in that day's tab at about 10 credits. Bethel was 7.61 credits. The military tactics and coalitions packets, written in one run, were 7.28 together. The library catalog and course report were 10.04. A follow-up review of books added to the library after the coalitions packet went out was 3.83. That is about 39 credits for the set. The Speedway packet came out of the Speedway revision run, which is priced with that revision. A credit is 1 US dollar of API list price, not what my subscription bills, priced with the same usage method as my other posts. Not counted: the coordinating session, which I don't price per task, writing this post, and my time.

## What I'd carry

- **Move the argument about sources to before the build.** It is cheapest there.
- **"What I could not open" is a deliverable.** Ask for it by name.
- **Read the copyright page, not the filename.** The edition is what the book says it is.
- **A source the course would use to teach checking is not a source the course cites.**
