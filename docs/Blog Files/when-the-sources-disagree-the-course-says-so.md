# When the Sources Disagree, My Course Says So

*Content note: this post names a 1930 lynching and drownings at a city beach, by place and year only. The courses it describes include them with content notes and no images.*

On November 1 I asked Claude to research places Black Americans vacationed, visited and built, starting with Idlewild in Michigan and Fox Lake near Angola, Indiana, for a series on Learn.WitUS. Later the same day I added Belmont Beach in Indianapolis. By that night there was a series plan, two new courses, and a third course corrected because one of its claims could no longer be sourced.

None of the new courses is public. That is on purpose, and it is most of what this post is about.

## The series

It is called **A Place of Our Own: Black Resorts, Retreats and Vacation Towns**. It starts from a course already on Learn.WitUS, *Where We Rested*, which teaches five places Black Americans owned: Highland Beach, Idlewild, American Beach, Oak Bluffs and Bruce's Beach. Before planning anything, Claude searched the whole catalog. Idlewild was covered. Fox Lake was not mentioned anywhere.

So the series adds courses for what *Where We Rested* leaves out:

- **Fox Lake: Indiana's Lake of Its Own**, 22 lessons, built.
- **Belmont Beach and the City's Water: Assigned, Claimed and Contested**, 29 lessons, built.
- **Idlewild after 1921**, a **Midwest ring** of lake communities, and a course on **mountain, Gulf and shore** resorts: approved, not built.

## Fox Lake: one question, four answers

In 1924 five businessmen from Fort Wayne, Marion and Kokomo bought the Ward farm on Fox Lake and incorporated the Fox Lake Land Company to sell lake lots to African Americans (Garner, 2025, p. 25). Indiana's statewide Green Book survey calls that the usual pattern: "The land for Black resorts was typically purchased by white businessmen, platted, and sold to Black buyers" (Borland & Linebarger, 2025, p. 103). Black buyers bought the lots and built the first cottages, and from 1943 two Black women, Mary Frances Fleming and Mabel Van Cleave, ran the hotel and restaurant (Garner, 2025, p. 28). The 2026 nomination calls Fox Lake "Indiana's only African American lake resort community" (Garner, 2025, p. 22).

Now ask when Fox Lake was founded. The sources give at least four answers, and the course teaches all four as different events:

| Answer | What it dates |
|---|---|
| 1924 | The land company buys the farm and incorporates (Garner, 2025, p. 25) |
| 1926 or 1927 | The plat is laid out and lots begin to sell (Garner, 2025, p. 25; Greiff, 2001, Sec. 8 p. 6) |
| 1928 | Carl Wilson Sr., a Fort Wayne businessman, starts the first cottage (Greiff, 2001, Sec. 8 p. 6) |
| 1927 | The National Park Service's theme study, counting from Black families renting cabins or building on lots they bought (Kahrl & Cammeron, 2022, p. 56) |

The plat is a real disagreement. The 2026 nomination says "Fox Lake's plat was established by 1926" (Garner, 2025, p. 25). Two pages earlier, the same form says the community "was established by 1927 when the original plat was created" (Garner, 2025, p. 23). The 2001 nomination says the first road was cut and the original plat subdivided in 1927 (Greiff, 2001, Sec. 8 pp. 5 to 6).

A course could pick one and move on. Ours says "1926 or 1927, and here is who says which," then names what would settle it: the plat as recorded in Steuben County. Nobody on this project has read that page yet, so the course does not pretend to know.

## Two claims the course will not teach

Some disagreements are not open. One source is simply better.

- Indiana Landmarks wrote that Fox Lake is "believed to be one of only two still-standing African American lake resorts in the nation, along with Idlewild in Michigan" (Indiana Landmarks, 2021). The theme study names Lake Ivanhoe in Wisconsin, Lake Placid in Missouri and Lake Adney in Minnesota as other Midwest lake communities that kept their Black identity after Jim Crow (Kahrl & Cammeron, 2022, p. 142). The course leaves the "one of only two" claim out and explains why.
- The theme study's own footnote says "Fox Lake was the first African American resort community listed on the National Register of Historic Places" (Kahrl & Cammeron, 2022, p. 56, n. 95). The same study lists Idlewild on the Register in 1979 and Fox Lake in 2001 (Kahrl & Cammeron, 2022, pp. 56, 188). That claim is out too.

## Belmont Beach: assigned, not owned

Belmont Beach needed its own course because it is the opposite case. *Where We Rested* is about places Black Americans owned so they would not have to ask. Belmont was city land.

The city's parks and beaches "were prohibited from use by Blacks" (Garner, 2024, Sec. 8 p. 14). Black west siders swam from a sandbar on the White River anyway, and on August 8, 1936 the *Indianapolis Recorder* announced a "new swimming beach for colored people at Belmont and White River," as the 2024 nomination quotes it (Garner, 2024, Sec. 8 p. 15). When a group led by Henry Fleming tried to buy riverfront land for a country club in 1927, "the Parks Board threatened to condemn the property if it was sold to African Americans" (Mullins, 2016).

The course has one hard rule about this place: it never calls Belmont Black-owned, because the record says otherwise. It also teaches the gaps. The nomination finds no Indianapolis newspaper reference to the beach after 1943 (Garner, 2024, Sec. 8 p. 17). The form nominated "Belmont Park & Beach," and the state's record shows the site listed on July 31, 2026 as "Belmont Beach" (Indiana Historic Preservation Review Board, 2026). Whether the park was dropped from the final boundary is a question the course asks out loud.

## Hard history, handled on purpose

The 2026 Fox Lake nomination puts the land company's Marion office and the 1930 lynching in Marion in the same sentence (Garner, 2025, p. 25). I decided the course would include it, briefly, with a content note and no images. The course also says plainly that no source connects the lynching to anything the company decided. Belmont's course includes drownings, and violence over pools and beaches in other cities, the same way: a content note, counts only, no images, no private victim named.

Two more rules came from me and apply to every course in the series. No living private person is named or quoted without their consent, even when a nomination quotes them. And no course reproduces the street addresses the 2026 nomination lists for every cottage, many under a family's name. The places are lived in.

## The check before the review

Before anyone outside reads these courses, a helper agent audited each one claim by claim against the sources, fetched again that day. It made about 45 fixes in Fox Lake and about 75 in the lessons of *Where We Rested* changed that day, from wrong page numbers to links that no longer opened.

The most important find was in the course that was already public. *Where We Rested* told the story of an Idlewild lot owners' association founded in 1921, citing the community's website. That page no longer mentions it, and no other source we could read does. The account is now labeled unsourced, its quiz question was rewritten, and the question went into the review request for the people who would know.

## Who reads it first

Each course stays unpublished until the community it describes has had about three weeks to read it, with authority to change or cut: the Fox Lake Preservation Foundation for Fox Lake, the Friends of Belmont Beach for Belmont, and the Idlewild Historic & Cultural Center for the Idlewild lessons. Claude drafted the requests. I send them myself.

With each request goes a **sources packet**: every source the course cites, the lessons it supports, its page or link, how to reach it, and every open conflict. A reviewer I invite as an auditor can also check every citation on the course's citation page. Some sources only a person can reach. The *Recorder* pages the nominations quote would not open for an automated reader, so the courses quote them only as the nominations do, and say so. Those pages are on my list.

## What I would tell someone building history courses with AI

- **Name the event before the date.** Half of the founding-date disputes I have seen are about different events.
- **When sources disagree, give both with their owners, then say what would settle it.** That sentence is a finding, not a weakness.
- **A better source can retire a claim.** Say which one, and why.
- **Audit against the sources, not against the draft.** The worst error was in a course that was already live.
- **The people the history belongs to read it first.** A three-week wait costs nothing next to a course that gets a community wrong.
