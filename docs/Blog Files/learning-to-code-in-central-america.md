# Five Months in Central America Did Not Make Me a Developer

From mid-June to early December 2017, I lived in Central America so I could learn to code full time. Five months. I was working on React, Node.js, Express, and auth, and I came home able to build and ship things. I did not come home an expert, and I want to be precise about that, because the version of this story that gets told at conferences usually skips it.

Five months bought me a floor. The eight years of building since is where everything else came from. If you are thinking about making the same jump, the floor is worth having, and it is smaller than it sounds.

I do not have a computer science degree. I still do not.

## The decision was arithmetic

People hear "moved abroad to learn to code" and imagine a decision about courage. It was a decision about a spreadsheet.

Here is the actual problem. Learning to build software takes months of concentrated time. Most people get that time one of two ways: an employer pays them to learn on the job, or they buy the time themselves. Nobody was paying me to learn React, so I had to buy the months, and the price of a month is your cost of living.

That gives you two levers. Raise the money, or lower the price. Raising the money was slow. Lowering the price was something I could do in a few weeks. Cutting what a month cost me is what turned full-time learning from impossible into a plan with an end date.

That is the whole insight, and it is not romantic. Nobody needs a plane ticket to learn React. What I needed was for the months to be cheap enough that I could spend them.

## What five months actually bought

By December I could build a full-stack JavaScript application: a React front end, a Node and Express API behind it, and authentication that worked. I understood ES6 well enough to read other people's code without stopping every third line.

That is a real floor and I do not want to talk it down. It is also not seniority. Five months of study does not give you production judgment, and it does not give you the thing that only shows up later: having been wrong before, in a specific way, and remembering the shape of it.

What five months gives you is permission to start building things nobody asked for, and that turns out to be the important part. Today I build and operate 21 interlinked products solo, including five AI agents in TypeScript, a Model Context Protocol server, and a property-based evaluation harness. None of that came from 2017. All of it was possible because of 2017.

## Teaching across a language gap is documentation training

While I was there, I taught full-stack JavaScript and React as a volunteer. My students were primarily Spanish speakers. My Spanish was not good enough to teach in.

So I could not explain my way out of a bad explanation. That is the whole lesson, and it took about one session to learn it.

When you and your students share a language, a weak diagram survives, because you patch it with sentences. You say "well, what I mean here is," and you talk for two minutes, and the confusion clears, and you never find out that the diagram was doing none of the work. Take the sentences away and the diagram has to carry the meaning by itself. It either does or it does not, and you find out in four seconds by looking at faces.

I taught with screenshots and diagrams. Annotated screenshots of the actual editor, the actual terminal, the actual browser, with arrows to the exact thing I meant. Diagrams of where a request goes and what comes back.

I still write technical documentation that way, and I write a lot of it. The habit that stuck: draw the thing first, then write the prose, and if the prose is holding the diagram up, the diagram is wrong. When I document an auth flow now, I assume the reader will skim the words and study the picture, because that is what people actually do. Teaching people who could not lean on my sentences taught me not to lean on them either.

## Teach What You Know, within 30 days

I was active in international FreeCodeCamp chapters while I was living there. I am an admin of PhxJS, and I have led FreeCodeCamp chapters in San Francisco, Phoenix, and Indianapolis, 100 or more active developers across them over the years.

Out of that I built a model I called "Teach What You Know." The rule: every developer teaches the module they just cleared, within 30 days of clearing it.

Two things make it work.

The person one step ahead is often the better teacher. They still remember the confusion. They know which sentence in the docs is the one that reads fine and means nothing until you have already understood it. An expert has lost that. The expert's explanation is correct and starts three steps past where you are standing.

The 30-day window is the part people skip, and it is the part that matters. Knowledge does not just fade, it smooths over. Past a month you remember that you learned the thing, and you have forgotten what was hard about it. You will teach a clean version of a messy experience, and the clean version does not help anybody. So you teach it while the mess is still fresh, or you have missed your window.

There is a selfish version of this too. Teaching a module 30 days after you cleared it is the fastest way to find out you did not actually clear it.

## The hour with no slides

The artifact from the trip came about ten months after I got back.

In October 2018 I gave a talk at Desert Code Camp called "How I overcame my fears and just learned React already. 5 Month React Journey Through Central America." JavaScript and Node.js track, room IRN-229, 11:00 AM, 37 people registered interest. I covered JavaScript, React, ES6, auth, and living abroad while learning.

The venue had no adapter that fit my laptop. My slides never went up on the screen. I gave the full hour with nothing but a room and my voice.

I got through it for reasons that had nothing to do with software. I am improv trained, and by then I had close to twenty years working crew on live sports broadcasts. Live crew work teaches you one thing above all: the show goes out either way. Equipment fails during the show, and the failure is not the emergency, the freezing is. So when the adapter did not fit, my body already knew what that was. Not a disaster, just Tuesday.

What I learned that day, and what I would hand to anyone about to present: the talk is the structure you are holding, not the file you are projecting. If you cannot deliver it without the deck, you do not have a talk yet, you have a slideshow with narration. The slides were a nice-to-have I never got. The hour was fine.

## What it cost, honestly

I owe you the other column.

There was no cohort. Nobody was on the same schedule as me, nobody noticed if I skipped a week, and the only accountability was mine. There were no career services, no employer partners, no one making introductions on my behalf. There is a gap on my resume where those months sit, with no company name to put in it, and you explain that gap to strangers for a long time afterward.

And there were stretches, weeks of them, where the only evidence that I was making progress was code nobody had asked for and nobody was going to see. No grade, no green check, no manager saying it looked good. Just you deciding whether last week counted. That is the part people underrate when they plan a jump like this, and it is the part that ends most of them.

## If you are considering the same jump

Do the arithmetic first. How many months do you need, and what does a month cost you where you are now? If cutting the cost of a month is what makes the plan possible, that is a legitimate reason to move, and a better one than wanting the experience.

Then be honest about what the months buy. Five months got me to the floor. Everything above it came from the years of building afterward, and there was no shortcut hiding in the plane ticket.

Teach while you learn. Teach the thing you cleared last month, before you forget what was hard. And whatever you build during the months nobody is watching, keep it. It is the only proof you are going to have.
