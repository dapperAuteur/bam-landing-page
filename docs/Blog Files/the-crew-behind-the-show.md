# The Crew Behind the Show: Three Courses I Will Not Release on My Own Word

Most of the people who make a show work are people the audience never sees. Three new courses are about them, in a series called The Crew Behind the Show: rigging, sound and light, for stage, set and broadcast crews.

All three are written, verified and merged. None is released. This post is about why.

## What was built

| Course | Sections | Lessons | Claim fixes | Quiz fixes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rigging for Stage and Broadcast Crews | 6 | 20 | 72 | 48 |
| Sound Craft | 7 | 26 | 88 | 58 |
| Light on Set | 8 | 27 | 78 | 56 |

Between them they carry nearly 1,700 quiz questions.

Each course was written from a brief I approved on 2026-10-08 and a research file in which every line is marked verified, derived, reported or unverified. Only the verified lines are asserted. Derived lines appear only with their reasoning shown in the lesson. Reported and unverified lines are left out, or named in the lesson as open questions.

Then came the checking: a checker for each section with a skeptic behind it, and a reviewer for each quiz bank with a skeptic behind that. The fix counts in the table are what that pass changed. Every hedge left in a lesson got a research check filed, 21 across the three courses, so each hedge has a record of what would settle it.

## Held until a named person says yes

Each course is public and held. Learners see a "Coming soon" page until a named, qualified reviewer has read every lesson marked for one, with authority to change it or cut it.

The markers are specific:

- In the rigging course, 17 of the 20 lessons need a qualified reviewer, for example an ETCP-certified rigger.
- Sound Craft's hearing lessons need an occupational-noise or hearing-conservation professional. Any description of a sound-making device needs a safety reviewer before it can become a build list. Its rights lessons need a rights or licensing reviewer.
- Light on Set needs an electrician or a stage lighting professional for the lessons near lamps, cords and heights.

Some lessons need a practitioner instead, someone who does the work. Part of the rigging course's second lesson will come from a recorded interview with me about my own broadcast utility work, checked by a qualified rigger before release. One Light on Set lesson waits for a gaffer and stays a page of links until then.

No reviewer is named yet for any of the three. That is the honest state of things. How those markers become a sign-off a reviewer can clear is in [the reviewer sign-off post](/blog/reviewer-name-on-what-they-read).

## What each course refuses to teach

The refusals are written as content, not as disclaimers.

**Rigging** is not a rigging qualification and never teaches anyone to fly a load. It never gives a capacity number. The rule about sling angles is taught as understanding, never as permission to rig, and a federal chain-sling table is shown as ratios rather than pounds. The copyrighted standards it refers to are named by title and linked to their listing pages, never to a PDF. None of the ANSI E1 entertainment standards was downloaded, because downloading one means accepting its license terms.

**Sound Craft** is not a hearing-conservation program, not legal advice on recording people, and not a build manual. It states no consent law anywhere and gives no target recording level. A 1913 book's stage recipes for thunder and artillery appear as history only, never as builds.

**Light on Set** gives no safe distance from a lamp, no load arithmetic and no modern illumination target, because no source in its research file states one. It draws a fence: a lighting hack works on the light after it leaves the lamp, at a distance, with something not attached to the lamp. Nothing touches, covers or wires into a fixture. The makeshifts some of its older sources describe are named as refused, never taught.

## Two deaths, taught from the investigations

I decided on 2026-10-08 that the rigging course would include two real fatality cases, with links to the official reports and to the news coverage.

Both are state investigations under the Fatality Assessment and Control Evaluation (FACE) programs: a stagehand who fell from the ceiling of an amphitheater, in California's report 12CA005 (California Fatality Assessment and Control Evaluation Program, 2014), and a rigging crew leader crushed by a light tower weight, in Massachusetts' report 19MA072 (Massachusetts Fatality Assessment and Control Evaluation Project, 2023).

Four rules shaped those two lessons:

- **The people who died are not named**, even where the press names them. The reports name no one, and the course follows the reports.
- **Linked, never hosted.** The reports and the news articles are linked, not copied into the course.
- **The matching is disclosed.** The reports name no venue. The course matched each one to its news coverage by date, place and event, and the lessons say that is how it was done.
- **When the news and the investigation disagree, the lesson teaches the disagreement.** In the California case, the day's news said the worker fell "from some scaffolding"; the investigation found he stepped from a duct onto a false ceiling panel. In the Massachusetts case, the day's news said the weight was 3,500 pounds; the report says 3,200. The facts come from the report.

That last rule is a small lesson of its own. A learner who cites the first-day story repeats its mistake. A rigging course is a good place to learn that the investigation outranks the headline.

## Three things worth stealing

**Write the hold before you write the lesson.** Every risky lesson starts with a line naming who must read it, and the course cannot be released around that line.

**Make refusals specific.** "No safe distance from a lamp, because no source states one" teaches more than "consult a professional."

**When you teach a real death, follow the investigation, not the coverage.** Name no one the report does not name, link instead of copying, and show learners where the first account and the finding disagree.
