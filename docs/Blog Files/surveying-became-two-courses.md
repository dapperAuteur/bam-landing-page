# One Surveying Course Became Two. Neither Will Say When Indiana's First Land Office Opened.

The third slot in [Tools That Think](/blog/tools-that-think) was supposed to hold one course, on how land gets measured. It now holds two, and both are written. This is about why I split it, the federal grid under Indiana, a date the courses refuse to state, and the two reviewers they are waiting for.

## The brief said one course. I said two.

The brief weighed a split and recommended against it. Its argument was a good one: the law about land boundaries is the answer to the error in measuring them.

The federal *Manual of Surveying Instructions* puts both halves on the page. Before 1900, most lines were measured with Gunter's chain, and "Such a chain was difficult to keep at standard length" (Bureau of Land Management [BLM], 2009, sec. 7-5, p. 165). The corners those chains set stand anyway: "The original corners shall stand as the true corners they were intended to represent, even though not exactly where professional care might have placed them in the first instance" (BLM, 2009, sec. 3-4, p. 40). Split the course, the brief warned, and the law half asserts a rule whose reason lives somewhere else.

I took the alternative anyway. The same brief had found that each half had enough verified material to stand alone, and the halves belong on different shelves.

- **Surveying: How Land Gets Measured** (Science & Math; 17 lessons, 446 quiz questions) is the tools: pacing, Gunter's chain, the surveyor's compass, the level, and area worked out from chains, from the coordinates of the corners, and with a polar planimeter.
- **Reading a Land Description: The Grid, the Deed and Indiana** (Civics, also Science & Math and Money & Property; 13 lessons, 360 quiz questions) is what those tools produced: the Land Ordinance of 1785 read as a primary source, townships, ranges and aliquot parts such as SW1/4NE1/4, metes and bounds, the deed, and Indiana's own survey.

The brief's warning shaped how they connect. The tools course teaches the errors that creep into a chained line, and its description says the law that grew around the old corners is in the companion course. The land-description course teaches that the corners stand anyway, and sends learners back to the tools course for the compass exercise below. Each course gives the other its reason.

## Indiana is counted from three starting lines

A township number looks like an address. In Indiana it is only half of one. The Manual's tables give three origins for the state's surveys: the Ohio River Base, begun in 1799, with townships counted north from the Ohio River; the Second Principal Meridian, adopted in 1805, for Illinois and Indiana; and the First Principal Meridian, adopted in 1819, for Ohio and Indiana (BLM, 2009, tables 3-1 and 3-2, pp. 41-42). A township number in Indiana means nothing until you know which line it counts from.

## A boundary turned by a needle

The oldest settled part of the state, the Vincennes Tract, was surveyed by Thomas Freeman. When the surveyor general, Jared Mansfield, reached Vincennes, he took astronomical observations at the tract's corners and found "that Freeman had run the north boundary on a magnetic bearing of N. 78° W. instead of true bearing and that the average magnetic declination was 6° 45' E" (White, 1983, p. 49). One unconverted bearing turned a whole boundary nearly 7 degrees off the line its description gave.

The tools course works that conversion, then sends the learner to NOAA's historic declination calculator to compare its value for Vincennes in 1804 with Mansfield's 6° 45' E. The lesson does not print the answer. In its own words, "an answer printed here before anyone has done it would only be a guess." A research check is filed to run it.

Even the year of Freeman's survey has two answers. The Bureau of Land Management's history says he "had already surveyed the boundaries of the Vincennes Tract in 1803" (White, 1983, p. 48). A 1916 article in the *Indiana Magazine of History* says "in 1802-3," and quotes Freeman's own record: "Crossed the Wabash July 21st (1802)" (Wilson, 1916, pp. 1, 12). The land-description course shows both and says what each one rests on.

## The date the courses will not state

When did Indiana's first land office open at Vincennes? The Indiana Archives and Records Administration answers twice on one page. In one paragraph: "The first land office in Indiana opened in 1807 at Vincennes." In a list of the offices "established in Indiana proper": "Vincennes (1804)" (Indiana Archives and Records Administration, n.d.). The Bureau of Land Management's history, describing the Act of March 26, 1804, adds that it "Establishes three new land offices at Vincennes, Detroit, and Kaskaskia" (White, 1983, p. 48).

Those may not even contradict each other. An act that establishes an office and the day the office opens for sales are different events. But the course does not know which one the 1804 means, so lesson 12 states neither year. It quotes both, explains the difference, and files a check asking for a primary record, such as the earliest entries in the Vincennes tract books or the appointments of the office's register and receiver. The check is rated medium, because the date matters to anyone dating a family's first land purchase from those books.

A guessed date in a civics course becomes a fact in somebody's family history. I would rather print the question.

## What the courses will not make you

Neither course makes anyone a surveyor, and neither ever tells a learner where a property line is. The land-description course gives no legal advice, and its exercises use a public building's record or a historical document, never a private home.

The compass is taught as the tool that made the old records, not the tool that settles a line today. On the methods for determining the direction of a line, the Manual is blunt: "Only the use of the needle compass is unequivocally prohibited" (BLM, 2009, sec. 2-4, p. 28).

## The two reviewers they wait for

Both courses are public, free and held as "Coming soon."

The tools course waits for a licensed land surveyor, still to be named, to read its two marked lessons: what the course will not make you, with what makes a survey legal, and the federal law protecting survey marks. It also waits for me to film ten demonstrations with a chain, a compass, a level and a planimeter.

The land-description course waits for the same surveyor on six lessons, with authority to change or cut them. Three of those six also wait for a Native reviewer, still to be named, with the same authority: the lessons on the Land Ordinance of 1785, the 1775 purchase from eleven Piankeshaw chiefs of land bounded in leagues along the Wabash, and how Indiana was surveyed. They teach that the grid followed treaty cessions. The Manual describes the 1804 act for the Indiana Territory as "an early example of the policy to extinguish Indian title to the public domain, followed by the extension of the surveys and settlement" (BLM, 2009, sec. 1-20(n), p. 21). A course that teaches that sentence should not be released on my reading of it alone. The course also waits for me to film a visit to a county surveyor's office.

Neither reviewer is named yet. Until they are, the lessons keep their markers and the courses stay held. The mechanics of a reviewer's sign-off are in [an earlier post](/blog/reviewer-name-on-what-they-read), and the same rule holds three crew courses back in [The Crew Behind the Show](/blog/the-crew-behind-the-show).

## Three things worth stealing

**When you overrule your own brief, keep its warning.** I split the course against the recommendation, and the recommendation's reason became the link between the two halves.

**Print the question, not the guess.** Two dates on one official page are a lesson in how records work. Picking one would put a guess into somebody's family history.

**Ask the people a history is about to read it.** The surveys followed the cessions. A course that teaches that waits for a Native reviewer, not only a surveyor.
